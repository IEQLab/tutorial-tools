Plant Over Its Life

Your cooling plant keeps emitting after the day it is commissioned. Refrigerant leaks from it every year, its efficiency depends on how it runs at part load, and it will be replaced two or three times before the building is. How much your refrigerant choice adds to the plant's emissions.

Every refrigerating system leaks a little each year, through joints, valves and servicing, and loses more when it is decommissioned. A refrigerant's global warming potential (GWP) says how many kilograms of CO2 one kilogram of it is equivalent to. So the same leak can be trivial or larger than a year of a small building's electricity, depending only on which refrigerant is in the machine. Low-GWP refrigerants bring their own constraints, which is why the safety class matters as much as the GWP.

The maths behind it

Annual leakage (t CO2-e) = number of units × charge (kg) × leak rate × GWP ÷ 1000.

End-of-life loss = total charge × share of charge still in the machine at end of life × (1 − recovery rate) × GWP ÷ 1000. The share left at end of life is 85% for chillers and 80% for VRF, from Cold Hard Facts 2022.

Over one plant life = annual leakage × plant life + end-of-life loss. The electricity comparison uses today's scope 2 factor for every year. Australia's grid is getting cleaner, so over 20 to 25 years the electricity figure is an overestimate and refrigerant's share will grow. That is an argument for low GWP, not against it.

GWP values are the 100-year AR4 values that Australian law uses (the Ozone Protection and Synthetic Greenhouse Gas Management Act 1989). Refrigerants that DCCEEW lists only as “less than 5” are calculated at 5, an upper bound; CO2 is 1 by definition. Two refrigerants are not on the DCCEEW list: R-1233zd(E) (GWP about 1, IPCC AR5) and the legacy HCFC R-22 and R-123 (IPCC AR4).

What this leaves out: a refrigerant change usually changes the efficiency and the charge too. A low-GWP choice that costs a few per cent of efficiency adds electricity emissions every year, so check the manufacturer's data before claiming the whole saving.

How efficient real chillers are, at full load and across the year.

A chiller's COP is measured at its full rated load, which it almost never runs at. The IPLV weights four part-load points by how many hours a typical plant spends at each (1% of hours at 100% load, 42% at 75%, 45% at 50%, 12% at 25%), so it is the better guide to annual energy. Every chiller sold in Australia is registered with both numbers under the GEMS Act. Each dot below is one of them.

Table view and the maths behind it

IPLV = 1 ÷ (0.01÷A + 0.42÷B + 0.45÷C + 0.12÷D), where A to D are the efficiencies at 100, 75, 50 and 25% load (the AHRI 550/590 weights).

For the same cooling delivered, compressor electricity scales with 1 ÷ efficiency, so the ratio of two medians gives the rough size of the gap. The ratings are not like for like on heat rejection. An air-cooled rating includes its condenser fans. A water-cooled rating stops at the chiller, so the cooling tower fans and condenser water pumps come on top, and so does the water the tower evaporates.

Source: GEMS registration database, chiller product type, exported 30 September 2026. Chillers registered as sold in Australia only (199 of 204). The export includes some registrations for products no longer available. Nothing on the register is below about 350 kWr.

How long the plant lasts, and what keeping it running legally involves.

Cooling plant usually lasts 20 to 25 years and a building lasts 50 or more, so the plant you propose will be replaced at least once. Each replacement brings a new refrigerant charge, new embodied carbon and a crane lift, which is why a replacement route belongs in the plant-room layout. Different plant also brings different maintenance duties, and some of them are set by law.
