Psychrometric Chart for HVAC Design

A psychrometric chart set up for the questions this unit asks of it. Load the weather file your DesignBuilder model uses and every hour of the year lands on the chart, sorted by what the outside air would have to go through to become comfortable. The chart reads the climate; deciding what that means for your building is yours.

What the outside air needs is not what your building needs.

Any two properties fix a state of air, so one point on this chart is one hour of weather, and a weather file is 8,760 of them. Each hour is sorted by what the outside air would have to go through to reach the comfort band: heating (right), sensible cooling (left), cooling and dehumidifying (down and left, through a coil colder than the air's dew point) or humidifying (up). That is what an air-handling unit does to the outside air it brings in.

The building is a different question. People, lights, equipment and sun keep adding heat indoors, so an office stays warm enough on its own until the weather drops below its balance point, which sits well below the heating setpoint. Between the two, the outside air is too cold to supply as it is, yet the building needs cooling, and that cool air can provide it: free cooling. So read the cold hours twice, once for the air and once for the building. More internal heat moves the balance point down and turns more of them into free cooling.

The balance point on the chart is a line to move, not a calculation. Your building's own depends on its gains and its envelope, which is what DesignBuilder works out.

2. The comfort band

The band is a design decision: the heating and cooling setpoints set its edges, the gap between them is the dead band, and the humidity limit sizes the coil. Set the band your brief would set.

Bands to start from:

Below the balance point the building needs heat; above it, the heat from people, lights and sun is more than the building loses. Leave it empty to hide it.

The maths behind the chart

Every property comes from the ASHRAE Handbook: Fundamentals, chapter 1, using the perfect-gas relations, which stay within 0.7% of the full real-gas formulation between −50 and 50 °C.

saturation pressure p_ws(t): Hyland–Wexler, over ice below 0 °C and over water above humidity ratio W = 0.621945 · p_w / (p − p_w) kg water per kg dry air relative humidity RH = p_w / p_ws(t) enthalpy h = 1.006 t + W (2501 + 1.86 t) kJ per kg dry air wet bulb t* W = ((2501 − 2.326 t*) W_s(t*) − 1.006 (t − t*)) / (2501 + 1.86 t − 4.186 t*) dew point the temperature at which p_ws equals the air's own p_w pressure at altitude p = 101.325 (1 − 2.25577×10⁻⁵ Z)^5.2559 kPa, Z in metres

One chart, one pressure. The chart is drawn at the standard pressure for the weather station's elevation, read from the file (101.325 kPa before a file is loaded). Each hour is placed by its dry bulb and dew point. The dew point fixes how much water the air holds whatever the pressure, which is why it is the second coordinate carried from the file. Plotting each hour at its own barometric pressure would put some points above the saturation curve drawn here.

How an hour is sorted. Moisture first, because only a coil colder than the air's dew point can take water out. If the hour holds more water than the wettest air in the band, it needs cooling and dehumidifying. If it holds less than the driest air in the band, it needs humidifying (and usually heating). Otherwise its moisture is already acceptable and a horizontal move brings it in: heating if it sits left of the band at that humidity ratio, sensible cooling if right. With a relative-humidity limit the band's edges lean, so the test uses the band's width at the hour's own humidity ratio rather than the setpoints alone.

The balance point. The outdoor temperature at which the heat a building gains from people, lights, equipment and sun equals what it loses through its envelope and its outside air. Below it the building needs heating; above it, cooling. Here it is an illustrative vertical line: hours that the outside air needs heated, but that lie above the line, are counted as free cooling for the building. No load is calculated.

Occupied hours. Weekdays, 08:00 to 18:00, with the day of the week taken from the weather file's own calendar. Public holidays are not removed. A weather file's hour 9 is the hour ending at 09:00, so this counts file hours 9 to 18.

The outside condition the plant is sized for, and what the plant has to do to it.

A design day is not the hottest hour in the file. It is a percentile: the 0.4% cooling condition is exceeded in about 35 hours of a typical year, so the plant copes with every other hour and falls short in those few. The arrow from that outside state to the edge of the band is the job the cooling plant is sized for. Across is heat to remove (the sensible leg); down is water to condense (the latent leg).

Two sets of numbers sit side by side here. The ASHRAE design conditions come from many years of records at the station and travel in the weather file's header; simulation tools build their design days from tables like these. The same rule applied to the file's own 8,760 hours gives a second answer, because a typical year is stitched together from typical months rather than being the long record.

The maths behind the design day

From the hours. Sort the year's dry bulbs from hottest down; the 0.4% value is the 35th (0.4% of 8,760). Its coincident wet bulb is the mean wet bulb of every hour within half a degree of that dry bulb. The dehumidifying condition does the same with dew point, carrying the mean coincident dry bulb. The 99.6% heating value is the 35th coldest hour. These follow the definitions of the ASHRAE Fundamentals climatic design tables, applied to one typical year instead of the multi-year record.

The arrow. It runs from the outside design state to the band's corner at the cooling setpoint and the upper humidity limit, or straight across if the outside air is already drier than the limit. The share of the work that is drying is the enthalpy drop across the latent leg over the drop across the whole arrow, using h = 1.006 t + W (2501 + 1.86 t). It is per kilogram of outside air: how much outside air there is, and what the room adds, is what a load calculation settles.

Psychrometric properties from the ASHRAE Handbook: Fundamentals, chapter 1 (perfect-gas relations; Hyland–Wexler saturation pressure), checked against the handbook's moist-air tables and worked examples. The chart lines follow the lecture's chart style, and the process colours follow the BPSD6030 drawing conventions shared with the lecture figures and the other BPSD6030 tools.
