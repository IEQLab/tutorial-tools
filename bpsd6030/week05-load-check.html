Load Sense-Check

A quick check that the peak heating and cooling loads from your DesignBuilder design run are in a believable range, and that you are reporting the figure Assignment 1 asks for. It takes about ten minutes, on your own or with a partner. It opens on a worked example: press Clear and type your own numbers.

Turn your peak loads into watts per square metre, and see whether they are believable.

A load in kilowatts means nothing until you know the building: 150 kW is enormous for a house and small for a hospital. Divided by the conditioned floor area (the floor area of the zones that are heated and cooled), it becomes a W/m² figure you can set against published values for the same uses. Those values are a check for gross errors, not a target: a figure far outside them points to something in the model to look at, and a figure inside them is not proof the model is right.

Two things to report the way Assignment 1 asks. First, the coincident block (whole-building) load, “the building’s load at its own peak”. The “Totals” row of DesignBuilder’s zone table is the sum of zone peaks: it adds each zone’s own peak, and those happen at different hours, so it is not the block load. Second, keep the calculated load and the design capacity (the load with DesignBuilder’s sizing factor applied) separate, say which you quote, and use the same basis for heating and cooling.

    How the check works

    W/m². Cooling W/m² = coincident peak cooling (kW) × 1000 ÷ conditioned floor area (m²). Heating is worked the same way. The conditioned floor area is the sum of the areas you enter for each use.

    Load or capacity. The comparison is made on the load. A figure entered as a design capacity is divided by DesignBuilder’s default sizing factor first: 1.15 for cooling and 1.25 for heating. If you changed the sizing factor in your model’s design calculation options, divide by your own value instead.

    The band for a mix of uses is area-weighted. For each use the tool holds a low and a high figure (the table below). With areas A1, A2, … the band’s high end is (A1 × high1 + A2 × high2 + …) ÷ (A1 + A2 + …), and the low end likewise. Floor area entered as “other conditioned use” has no published figure, so it is left out of the weighting and the band then covers only part of the building, which the result says. A figure more than one and a half times the band’s high end is called well above.

    The sum of zone peaks flag fires when your coincident peak cooling is within 1% of the sum of zone peaks. The coincident peak can equal the sum only if every zone peaks in the same hour, which is rare in a building with perimeter zones on different facades. A coincident figure more than 1% above the sum is also flagged, since a building cannot peak higher than its zones added together: the two are probably on different bases or from different runs. The check is for cooling only. Heating design in DesignBuilder is steady state (fixed outdoor conditions, no sun), so every zone peaks under the same conditions and the heating Totals row is the building’s heating load.

    The mixed-basis flag fires when cooling and heating are on different bases, one a load and the other a design capacity.

    Why heating has no band. The published per-area figures used here are for cooling. Howell gives heating figures per cubic metre of building for US winter design conditions, which do not transfer to an Australian climate, so the tool reports your heating W/m² for comparison and applies no band to it.

    The published figures, by use

    Preliminary cooling figures from two textbooks, converted to W/m² of floor area and rounded to the nearest 5. They are first-pass plant-capacity figures from US practice and they run high: Howell notes that crude rules of thumb “significantly oversized equipment and wasted energy”, and Grondzik and Kwok present theirs as preliminary estimates only. They do not separate floor positions: a top floor carries the roof and a ground floor the slab, so expect your own floors to spread around a single value.

    The office band (50–120 W/m²) is the working band this unit uses for an Australian office. It is the teaching team’s figure rather than a value from a published table: its high end sits below Howell’s perimeter figure, and its low end matches an intermediate office floor in the unit’s own loads-method worked sketch (50 W/m²). For every other use the high end is the higher of the two published figures, and the low end is 40 W/m², the lowest figure in either table (Grondzik and Kwok’s shaded-side apartment).

    Where the numbers come from, so you can check them and go further:

    • Grondzik, W. T. & Kwok, A. G. (2015). Mechanical and Electrical Equipment for Buildings (12th ed.). Wiley. Table 12.1, “Preliminary Estimates for Cooling Capacity”, p. 438, given as floor area per ton of refrigeration (1 ton = 3.514 kW).
    • Howell, R. H. (2017). Principles of Heating, Ventilating and Air Conditioning (8th ed.). ASHRAE. Table 1-1, “Typical Building Design Heat Losses or Gains”, p. 6, given as ft² per ton and m² per kW. The quotation on crude rules of thumb is from p. 5.
    • The office band of 50–120 W/m² is the BPSD6030 teaching team’s working band for an Australian office, not a published figure.
    • Sizing factors (1.15 cooling, 1.25 heating) are DesignBuilder’s defaults in its cooling and heating design calculation options; check your own model’s settings.
    • The worked example is the three-floor retail and office building from the BPSD6030 loads-method page (“Scaling your Assignment 1 loads”): ground-floor retail at 70 W/m², an intermediate office floor at 50 and a top office floor at 65, each 900 m². Its coincident peak (150 kW) and heating load (95 kW) are illustrative values added for this tool.
