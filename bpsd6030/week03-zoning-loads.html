Zoning and Heat Balance

One office floor plate, loaded on a hot Sydney summer day and a cold winter one. Tab 1 zones it three ways so you can see which numbers zoning moves. Tabs 2 and 3 split the building’s peak into its parts and let you test design changes against one base case. The numbers come from a simplified hourly load model inside this page, so they show patterns, not your building’s loads: DesignBuilder, running the EnergyPlus heat balance, is the authority on those. Nothing here asks for your own results.

Zone the same floor three ways and watch which numbers move.

A thermal zone is a part of the floor that one thermostat controls. On a deep floor plate, a perimeter zoneA strip of floor about 4 m deep behind a facade, drawn with a virtual partition (no wall, just a zone boundary). Facades facing different ways get separate zones because the sun reaches them at different times of day. is a strip about 4 m deep behind each facade, split by the way the facade faces, and the core is the interior behind it. Toilets, stairs, lifts, plant and storage go in one unconditioned zone: modelled, but not heated or cooled. The plate here is a five-storey office: L-shaped floors of 720 m², 16 m deep, zoned with 3.6 m perimeter zones, and a strip of toilets, lift and plant room along a party wall to the south.

Two totals matter. The sum of zone peaksEach zone’s highest load of the day, added up, whatever hour each one happens. It is what the “Totals” row of DesignBuilder’s zone table reports. It sizes nothing on its own. adds each zone’s own worst hour, and each zone’s air supply is sized on its own peak. The coincident peakThe whole building’s load at the single hour when that total is highest. Zones that peak at other hours are counted at what they need in that hour, not at their own peak. The central plant is sized on this. is the whole building’s load in its own worst hour, and the central plant is sized on that. Change the zoning and watch which of the two moves.

    How the loads are worked out

    The day. A clear-sky cooling design day for Sydney Airport in January. The hottest hour is the 0.4% design dry bulb, 33.6 °C, with a mean coincident wet bulb of 19.7 °C. The rest of the day follows ASHRAE’s daily range method: each hour sits a fixed fraction of a 10.8 K daily range below the peak, lowest near 05:00 and highest in the early afternoon. Sunshine comes from the ASHRAE clear-sky model at 34° S on 21 January, with the clear-sky optical depths tabulated for Sydney in that month. These are the inputs of the ASHRAE 0.4% cooling design day for Sydney Airport, the design day EnergyPlus and DesignBuilder read from the weather data. The 10.8 K is the mean daily range on days that reach the 0.4% condition; the weather file header also lists 6.9 K, which is the hottest month’s average daily range and a different quantity. Times are standard time; under daylight saving the clock reads an hour later.

    Each zone, each hour. Sun through glass = glass area × SHGC × sunshine on the facade, with the direct part reduced at steep angles of incidence. Glass conduction = U-value × area × (outdoor − indoor temperature). Walls and roof use the sol-air temperatureThe outdoor temperature that would push the same heat into a wall as the real air temperature plus the sunshine it absorbs. A dark roof in summer sun can sit 30 K or more above the air temperature. in place of the air temperature, and their heat arrives inside over several hours. People add 75 W sensible and 55 W latent (moisture) each at 10 m² a person; lights 4.5 W/m²; equipment 11 W/m², all at full value from 08:00 to 18:00. Outside air is 10 L/s a person while the system runs (07:00 to 18:00); infiltration is 0.75 air changes an hour of the office volume, shared between the perimeter zones by facade length.

    Thermal mass. Heat that arrives as radiation (sun on the floor, much of the heat from people and lights) warms the room’s surfaces first and reaches the air over the next few hours. The tool spreads it with ASHRAE’s radiant time series for a medium-weight, carpeted office (about half of it in the first hour, most of the rest over the next five), and delays wall and roof conduction with ASHRAE’s conduction time series for a brick-veneer wall and an insulated concrete roof. That is why the peaks sit later than the sun.

    Heating. Steady state at the 99.6% winter design temperature, 6.9 °C, against 21 °C inside: conduction through glass, walls and roof, plus outside air and infiltration. No sun and no internal gains, as in DesignBuilder’s heating design calculation.

    Loads, not capacities. Everything here is a calculated load. DesignBuilder’s design capacity multiplies the load by a sizing factor; none is applied.

    Why the coincident peak does not move here

    In this model a zone boundary is a line on the plan: the same sun, people, lights and outside air are in the building however it is divided, so the building’s hour-by-hour load is identical under the first two zonings and the coincident peak cannot change. In DesignBuilder it moves a little, typically a few per cent, because zones there exchange heat across their boundaries and each zone’s air temperature is controlled separately.

    The sum of zone peaks does change, and by a lot, because splitting lets each facade reach its own worst hour. That is why it is not the block load: report the coincident figure as the building’s load and keep the zone peaks for sizing each zone’s supply.

    What this model leaves out

    It is a simplified hourly model with a stated method, not a simulation. It holds every room at its setpoint all day, treats the floors, ceilings and the party wall as adiabatic (no heat across them), ignores the ground slab and any shade from neighbouring buildings, and takes the outdoor air as drier than the room on this design day, so outside air adds no latent load (a humid day would). Your building’s loads come from DesignBuilder.

    Find the biggest part of the peak before you try to shrink it.

    A heat balance splits a load into the paths the heat takes: sun through the glass, heat conducted through glass, walls and roof, the people, lights and equipment inside, the outside air the system brings in for ventilation, and the infiltration that leaks in through the facade. A design change only moves the peak much if it acts on a large component.

    Cooling is read at the building’s coincident peak hour. Heating is a different calculation, steady winter conditions with no sun and nobody inside, so only conduction, outside air and infiltration count. The largest component is often not the same for both. This tab uses the perimeter and core zoning, with the services unconditioned.

      What each component is

      Sun through glass: sunshine transmitted by the glazing, set by its area and its SHGCSolar heat gain coefficient: the share of the sunshine falling on the glass that ends up as heat inside, from 0 to 1. Clear double glazing is about 0.7; a solar-control low-e glass can be 0.3 or less., and by the way the facade faces. Glass conduction, walls and roof: heat driven by the temperature difference, set by each element’s U-valueHeat flow through one square metre of an element for each degree of temperature difference, in W/m²K. Lower is better insulated.; only the top floor has a roof. People include their moisture (latent heat). Outside air is the ventilation air brought to room conditions. Infiltration is unplanned leakage through the facade.

      Why cooling is read at one hour. The parts of the cooling load peak at different times: the west glass late in the afternoon, the roof later still, the people from mid-morning. The plant meets the building at its coincident peak hour, so that is the hour whose parts a design change has to reduce. The zone table on this tab is also read at that hour, not at each zone’s own peak.

      DesignBuilder’s version. Its cooling and heating design results list similar components for each zone and for the building. The names differ a little (glazing, walls, roofs, ventilation, infiltration and so on), and the reading is the same: find the biggest, then act on it.

      Change one thing at a time against one base case, and log each step.

      Each logged step is measured two ways: from the step before it (the increment) and from the base case (the cumulative reduction). Read down the log and it is a route, not a set of separate tests. The dashed line is a 15% cut in the coincident design loads, the Assignment 1 target.

      Before each change, check which component is largest now. It shifts as you go: shrink one and another takes its place.

      How the log adds up

      Increment = load after the previous step − load after this step. Cumulative = (base-case load − load after this step) ÷ base-case load. Increments do not simply add: two measures that act on the same component (a lower SHGC and an overhang both cut sun through glass) each have less left to cut when the other is already in, which is why the log has to record the order.

      The loads tracked are the coincident cooling peak and the heating load of the whole building, perimeter and core zoning, services unconditioned. If a change moves the peak hour, the cooling figure is read at the new hour.

      What each control stands for in DesignBuilder

      Window-to-wall ratio, SHGC and glass U-value: the glazing template’s percentage and the simple glazing definition (U-value, SHGC). Overhang: local shading, a horizontal overhang at the window head. Orientation: the site orientation, turning the whole building. Wall and roof U-values: the construction build-ups. Lighting power density: lighting, normalised power density. Infiltration: the constant infiltration rate in air changes an hour. Heat recovery: on the HVAC tab, sensible heat recovery on the outside air.

      Things this page holds fixed: occupancy, equipment and the outside-air rate (10 L/s a person, a ventilation minimum, so cutting it is not a design option), the setpoints (24 °C cooling, 21 °C heating), the weather and the constructions’ thermal mass.

      Where the numbers come from, so you can check them and go further:

      • Plate: a five-storey office, L-shaped floors of 720 m² with 3.6 m perimeter zones, a services strip of toilets, lift and plant room, and a party wall to the south, treated as adiabatic.
      • Design conditions: ASHRAE (2025), Handbook—Fundamentals, ch. 14, Climatic Design Information, Sydney Intl Airport (WMO 947670): the 0.4% cooling design day (21 January; 33.6 °C dry bulb, 19.7 °C mean coincident wet bulb, 10.8 K mean coincident daily range; ASHRAE 2017 clear-sky model with τb 0.417 and τd 2.373) and the 99.6% heating dry bulb (6.9 °C), as written to the design-day file that accompanies the OneBuilding TMYx 2011–2025 weather file for the station.
      • Daily range fractions, clear-sky solar model and 21 January solar constants: ASHRAE Fundamentals, ch. 14.
      • Load method: ASHRAE Fundamentals, ch. 18, Nonresidential Cooling and Heating Load Calculations (radiant time series), as reproduced in Howell, R. H. (2017), Principles of Heating, Ventilating and Air Conditioning (8th ed.), ASHRAE, ch. 7: daily range percentages (Table 7-2), radiant and convective splits (Table 7-29), wall conduction time series, wall 12 (Table 7-30), roof conduction time series, roof 14 (Table 7-31), non-solar and solar radiant time series, medium construction with carpet, 50% glass (Tables 7-33, 7-34), people at 75 W sensible and 55 W latent, “moderately active office work” (Table 7-14). Glass angular response: 6 mm clear double glazing, SHGC 0.70, 0.67, 0.64, 0.58, 0.45 and 0.23 at 0, 40, 50, 60, 70 and 80° incidence and 0.60 for diffuse light (Table 7-4 SI, row 5b, p. 281, reproducing ASHRAE Fundamentals ch. 15, Table 10).
      • Office defaults, as taught in this unit for DesignBuilder activity inputs: occupancy 10 m² a person (NCC 2019, Table D1.13); lighting 4.5 W/m² (NCC 2019, Table J6.2a, office); equipment 11 W/m² internal sensible heat gain (NCC 2022 Volume One, Specification 35, Table S35C2l, Class 5), the same specification’s Table S35C2n giving 75 W sensible and 55 W latent a person; outside air 10 L/s a person (AS 1668.2:2012, office). Infiltration 0.75 air changes an hour: 15 air changes an hour at 50 Pa (CSIRO, typical Australian construction) divided by 20, a common rule of thumb for natural conditions.
      • Base-case fabric (clear double glazing U 2.9, SHGC 0.70, 50% vision glass; walls U 0.60; roof U 0.45) and setpoints (24 °C cooling, 21 °C heating, a 3 K dead band against the NCC J6D3 minimum of 2 K) are illustrative values for an older office, chosen for this tool, not taken from a standard.
